I am an independent software developer building noncustodial account and

Tokenized Securities Venue, a liquidity provider, a broker-dealer, or an

and of published trade data bears directly on work I am doing, and because I
believe two narrow gaps are worth the Commission's attention before the
framework is extended or made durable.

My comments are limited to three points.

---

## 1. The framework does not address orders originated by software agents

The Order allocates relief along the axes of venue and liquidity provider. It
does not appear to address the case where the order submitted to a permissioned
AMM Liquidity Pool is generated by software acting under a delegated, scoped
permission rather than by a person at the moment of the trade.

This is not a hypothetical. Programmable accounts under ERC-4337 support
time-limited, scope-limited delegated keys, and a single Ethereum Layer 2 built
for tokenized assets reported over 123,000 such accounts and 1.6 million user
operations within roughly two months of mainnet launch. Several platforms have
also begun offering account features that connect automated agents to trading
infrastructure. Whatever the Commission's eventual view, a material share of
order flow reaching a TSV during this five-year period is likely to be
software-originated.

**Recommendation.** In any extension or durable rulemaking, the Commission
should state whether a TSV bears any obligation to distinguish
software-originated from human-originated order flow, and whether accepting
such flow affects a TSV's eligibility. Silence will be read by some
participants as permission and by others as prohibition, which is the worst of
both outcomes for a framework whose stated purpose is to generate usable data.

I express no view on how that question should be answered. I note only that
answering it explicitly is more useful than leaving it implied.

---

## 2. Published execution data without rejection data produces a biased record

The Order requires venues to make transaction data publicly available at
regular intervals, including price, size, time, pool address, end-of-day pool
size, and daily volume. This is a genuine improvement over the opacity of
offshore tokenized-equity products, and I support it.

However, publishing only executed trades produces a systematically incomplete
picture of how a venue behaves. Orders that are rejected — for exceeding a
pool's available depth, for breaching a venue risk limit, for arriving during a
halt, or for failing a pre-trade validation — are precisely the events that
reveal whether a venue's risk management functions under stress. A dataset
containing only what succeeded cannot answer the question the Commission says
it wants answered: how do these venues actually function?

The same asymmetry has a well-documented effect in performance reporting more
generally, where records that omit unsuccessful outcomes overstate results.

**Recommendation.** Require TSVs to publish, at the same interval and in the
same format, aggregate counts and reason codes for orders not executed,
categorized at minimum as: insufficient pool depth, venue risk limit breach,
volume cap reached, halt or suspension, and other. Aggregate counts with reason
codes impose modest burden, expose no individual participant, and would
substantially improve the evidentiary value of the pilot.

---

## 3. Risk-management conditions should specify deterministic pre-trade validation

The Order invites comment on the adequacy of risk management conditions for
permissioned AMMs. My observation is narrow and practical.

In automated trading systems, the durable safety property is not the quality of
the decision logic but the existence of a validation layer that is separate
from it, deterministic, and unable to be overridden by the component proposing
the trade. Where the proposing component is a statistical model, this
separation is the difference between a bounded system and an unbounded one. A
model's confidence in its own output is not a risk control.

**Recommendation.** To the extent the Commission specifies risk-management
conditions, it should express them as properties of a pre-trade validation
layer — that the checks execute deterministically before a trade reaches the
pool, that they cannot be disabled or parameterized by the order-originating
component, and that the venue can evidence both. Conditions framed as outcomes
rather than as architectural properties are difficult to audit and easy to
satisfy nominally.

I also note that volume and symbol caps function as a systemic risk control
rather than a venue-level one, and are not a substitute for per-order
validation at the venue.

---

## Closing

I appreciate the Commission's willingness to generate evidence through a
bounded, conditional framework rather than through enforcement, and I recognize
that the three points above concern matters at the edge of the Order's current
scope. I offer them because the framework's value depends on the data it
produces, and because the gaps I have described bear directly on whether that
data will support the conclusions drawn from it.

I would be glad to provide further detail on any of the above.

# Before you submit — read this

## This is permanent and public

Comment letters are published on sec.gov with your name and are indexed by
search engines. They do not come down. Consider:

- Do you want your name publicly attached to this project's subject matter
  before the project exists? There are reasons to say yes (credibility,
  a citable artifact for grant applications) and reasons to say no.
- The SEC publishes what you submit. Use an email address you are willing to
  have public, or check the current submission form for whether contact details
  can be withheld.
- Nothing in this letter should describe AgentVault by name or as a product
  offering. The current draft deliberately does not. Keep it that way — a
  comment letter that reads as promotion undermines its own point.
